Why it's priced for a project
This is the one listing in the set that says it out loud: "the home needs updating but offers a great starting point for those looking to bring their vision to life." A 1912 traditional with hot-water (radiator) heat and no air conditioning, dated kitchen and baths, on a 0.37-acre lot. The upside baked into the structure is unusual: a slate roof (expensive to replace, so a real asset if sound), a finished basement, and a walk-up, partially-finished attic — the listing flags "potential for up to four levels of living space."
Condition read (preliminary — verify)
On a 1912 home the inspection priorities are the slate roof (have a slate specialist confirm it's sound — repairs are costly but far cheaper than replacement), the hot-water heating (boiler age, radiator condition, and whether you add central A/C via high-velocity or mini-splits since there's no existing ductwork), and the usual century-home items — knob-and-tube or mixed wiring, galvanized plumbing, and any lead/asbestos in finishes. None of these are disclosed as problems; they're simply the places a 1912 budget gets made or broken. Commission a licensed inspector (and a slate-roof assessment) before any offer.

Ways to protect the margin
1. Prove the roof first. A sound slate roof is a selling point and saves a five-figure replacement; a failing one changes the whole deal. Get that answer before you're committed.
2. Finish what's already roughed in. The biggest cheap-sqft win is completing the attic and basement that are already partially finished — more countable area without moving walls.
3. Mind the taxes. At ~$7,200/yr the carrying cost is real on a longer reno; keep the timeline tight.
