Why it's the value-add play
A 1920 farmhouse with real character — original details, hardwood floors, an already-updated kitchen, a front porch and a big fenced yard on 0.45 acre. The listing calls it "move-in ready," so this isn't a gut; the value gap is narrower and structural: it has one full bath, and it's on the second floor, plus oil heat and window A/C. It last sold in April 2023 for $260,000, so the ask is only ~$30K above that and the price has already been cut $10K — a seller with limited room but some motivation. The flip here is adding a bath and modernizing systems, not rebuilding.
Condition read (preliminary — verify)
The defining limitation is the single second-floor bathroom — adding a main-level bath (or a primary bath) is the highest-return move and the first thing to scope. Beyond that it's standard 1920-cottage diligence: oil heat (boiler/tank age and whether to convert), window units vs. adding real cooling, and the usual look at wiring and plumbing age. The partial basement is unfinished. Nothing is disclosed as defective — confirm with a licensed inspection before any offer.

All-in (purchase + core reno): ~$362K–$436K → ARV ~$380K–$440K. A thin-margin, fast-turn flip rather than a run at the $600K tier — the math only works if you buy below ask and keep the scope disciplined.
Ways to protect the margin
1. Lead with the bath. Going from 1 to 2 baths is the biggest value unlock on a 3-bedroom home — scope it first and make sure a main-level location is feasible.
2. Don't over-improve. With the kitchen already updated and a ~$440K ceiling, keep finishes tasteful-but-mid-market; dollars spent above comp level won't come back.
3. Negotiate. One bath, oil heat and a recent price cut are all leverage — your margin is largely made at the purchase price here.
